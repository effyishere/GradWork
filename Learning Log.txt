11/04 - “Easy Facial Rigging and Animation Approaches,” Pedro Tavares Barata Bastos, 2015. Accessed: April 11, 2026. [Online]

"Facial rigging is the act of planning and building the deformation mechanics and the interactive control interfaces to animate the face of a 3D character. The process of planning and building these structures is carried out by riggers, who are the artists responsible for providing the animators with an articulated facial rig." <- Definition of facial rigging

"In addition, this field lacks advanced and complete documentation and the references available do not provide validation on how to build a facial rig. Instead, they are limited to isolated cases, which generally do not encompass dealing with more complex facial behaviours." <- About the lack of documentation on rigging

"The earliest attempt to determine the minimum number of polygons needed to model a realistic face established the use of 250 polygons [Parke 1972, Parke 1974]. Since then, this number has been gradually increasing due to the also gradual higher demand for better-looking facial models and also the evolution of computer graphics hardware" <- about early cases of topology

The paper highlights that mesh topology is crucial for proper deformation, regardless of the modelling technique. Edge loops are typically arranged based on facial anatomy to support realistic movement. It also notes that early approaches focused mainly on surface appearance rather than the underlying drivers of facial motion.

"In a character's body, the joints act as the links between the bones to allow bending and twisting the entire skeleton. In a character's face, the skeleton needs to be highly articulated, but it is generally not necessary for it to be similar to a real person's skeleton." <- About the positioning of joints in the face

"For the skeleton to work properly, the rigger uses constraints [Badler et al. 1986], which allow to connect and restrict the position, orientation and scale of the bones. Constraints limit the transformations of bones using non-hierarchical relationships [O'Neill 2008]. These are setup by the rigger with the particular goal to prevent the animator from doing unexpected manipulations of the rig controls, which could cause incorrect mechanical movements and awkward deformations" <- About constraints and parenting

"BTMs are duplicated facial models of an original one with equal topology [Maraffi 2003] but sculpted with a distinct shape in order to blend from the original to the duplicate via a weight parameter in the deformer." <- What are blendshapes

About UI: The paper discusses the concept of personalized facial rig controls, proposing a user-adapted interface that can respond to individual animator preferences. It highlights that traditional rig interfaces are often fixed and limited due to production constraints, resulting in less optimal workflows. A personalized system could reduce the need for repeated adjustments and improve both efficiency and usability.

"Skeletons and blendshapes are the most common techniques because they are the ones that artists use the most. The reasons why artists use them more is that (i) they perform efficiently, (ii) they integrate well with each other and (iii) they are available in the major 3D tools" <- About why joint and blendshape-based techniques are the most common

"PSD operates by storing multiple poses and interpolating between the poses according to the positioning of controls. [...] each pose of PSD identifies a pairing of a parametric location of a control to a set of values [...] these values indirectly influence the use of deformers, while others apply directly to the scaling of blendshapes. The blendshapes introduce refinements to the face that are either unattainable by the deformers alone. As the controls move, values are updated, and both deformers and blendshapes modify the face. 21/04 - https://www.wagnertechartist.com/post/creating-a-face-rig-with-joints-and-blend-shapes

Blend Shape–Based Deformation
Pros
- Easy to set up because it does not rely heavily on skin weighting
- Produces very clean, stable shapes that stay on model
- Extremely fast due to simple linear interpolation
- Efficient and performant for real-time or production use
Cons
- Limited flexibility because it relies on predefined shapes
- Poor handling of transitions and secondary animation
- Linear, additive nature can cause double transformations
- Difficult to achieve smooth, arcing motion between poses
- Requires additional systems (e.g., RBF solver) to improve interpolation

Joint-Based Deformation
Pros
- Smooth interpolation between poses due to 3D transformations
- Offers greater control over deformation through rig setup
- Supports secondary controls, improving animator flexibility
- More interactive and adjustable during animation
Cons
- Lower deformation fidelity compared to blend shapes
- Requires strong skin weighting skills to achieve good results
- Harder to match the precise, sculpted detail of blend shapes

Hybrid Approach
- Combines the high-quality deformation of blend shapes with the flexibility of joints
- Maintains clean, on-model shapes while allowing additional control layers
- Enables both precise sculpted expressions and dynamic adjustments
- Results in a more complete and animator-friendly facial rig

"Wrinkles are [...] a major sign of aging [...] and are important for understanding and interpreting facial expressions [...] There are two types of wrinkles: expressive wrinkles and micro wrinkles due to aging [...] Expressive wrinkles are caused by muscle movement and become more permanent due to changes in skin structure and elasticity [...] Aging causes elastic fibers to stretch and thin, collagen to cluster, and skin regeneration to slow, leading to micro furrows [...] These processes create two main characteristics: the micro furrow and the bulge of the wrinkle [...] Changes in the dermo-epidermic junction reduce skin elasticity [...] Expressive wrinkles are creases formed by muscle tension, and while their intensity varies, their shape and placement remain consistent [...] Wrinkles can therefore be linked to specific muscle actions and represented using a standardized reference wrinkle mask." <- about how wrinkles form and why they matter for facial animation.

"In 1978, Blinn introduced bump mapping, which consisted in modifying surface normals before lighting calculations to give a visual impression of wrinkles, without deforming the geometry. "Later in the mid 1970s, Ekman and Friesen [EF75] identified the six basic facial expressions: surprise, fear, disgust, anger, happiness and sadness. Soon after unraveling the main facial expressions, Ekman and Friesen [EF78] created FACS, the Facial Action Coding System. FACS is a manual that recognizes, describes and scores the muscular activity of the human face into Action Units (AUs), Action Descriptors (ADs) and Gross Behavior Codes (GBCs). It comprises the visible facial movements that produce momentary changes in facial appearance (either emotional or conversational signals). It is the largest and most accurate reference available regarding the study of the human facial behaviors. Therefore, FACS is a reliable reference to determine the categories in which to fit each facial behavior, without having to recognize what facial muscles are responsible for what movement." <- definition of FACS
